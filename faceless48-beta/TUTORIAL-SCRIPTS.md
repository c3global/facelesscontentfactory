# Faceless48 Creator: launch tutorial scripts 2 through 9

Tutorial 1 (Download, install and open it) was written earlier in this project. These are the other eight.

**How to read these.** Everything under "Say this" is spoken, in your voice, in plain words. Change anything that does not sound like you. "[pause]" marks a spot to click or wait on screen. "Have ready" is what to set up before you record. "I make" is what I produce with the plug-in so you only record your voice.

**Format key**
- **SCREEN** = you record your screen while you talk. I cut the waiting, add captions, zoom and callouts.
- **VOICE** = you record only your voice (or I use your approved voice clone). I render the visuals with the plug-in.

## Tutorial 2: Get me set up

**Format:** SCREEN, one real onboarding session. **Length:** about 10 minutes after I cut the waiting.

**Have ready**
- Your brand name, spelled the way you want it on screen.
- Your logo as a PNG with a transparent background, at least 1000 pixels wide.
- Your three brand colors as hex codes, or open Canva or your website so you can show finding them.
- A clip of yourself, if you want your caption preview to show your own face. A short one is fine.
- A clean assistant window, with nothing private showing.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. This is the video where you teach the plug-in who you are. You do it one time. After that, every video you make already looks like you.

Plan on 30 to 60 minutes for your real setup. I'm going to move faster here so you can see the whole shape of it.

You start with one sentence. Type: Get me set up. [pause] That's the whole command.

Your assistant asks about your business first. Your brand name, exactly as you want it on screen. A tagline, if you have one. And in a sentence or two, who your videos are for and what you help them with. [pause] It writes this down so it never asks again.

Next, your logo. The best file is a PNG with a transparent background. If your logo has a white box around it, the assistant will tell you, and you can keep it or swap in a better file. [pause] Copy the logo into your project's brand folder and tell the assistant the file name.

Now colors. You need three: a main color, a darker version, and your darkest one for backgrounds. They're written as hex codes, a pound sign and six characters. If you don't know yours, there are three easy places to look. In Canva, click a colored shape in a branded design and the code shows next to the color. On your website, right-click a colored button, choose Inspect, and the code is in there. Or give the assistant your logo and it will suggest colors from the file. It tells you those are suggestions, and nothing gets saved until you say yes. [pause]

Now your metallic finish. Your videos have a thin metal trim on the frames, lines and check marks. You choose gold, rose gold, silver or copper. [pause] The assistant shows you the choice so you aren't guessing from a word.

Fonts come next. You pick a pairing from the ones that come with the plug-in. If you have a brand font that isn't on the list, tell it. A custom font has to be added and tested, so it can't be promised on the spot.

Then it asks who is on screen. Your face. An AI avatar of you made in another tool. A cartoon or 3D character you own. The built-in stick figure. Or no one, just voiceover and graphics. You can pick more than one. If you bring your own character art, make sure you own the rights to it.

Here's my favorite part. The assistant makes one image that shows three caption styles side by side, in your colors. Editorial is elegant, with words arriving in polished lockups. Heavy is big and bold, built for social. Pill is classic subtitles in a rounded pill. [pause] Look at it, pick the one that feels like you, and say so. You can still ask for a different style on any single video later.

Last, your defaults. Portrait for Reels, TikTok and Shorts, landscape for YouTube, or both. Your talking-head style. Your explainer style. And whether you want background music. [pause]

Then it saves everything into two files: your brand file and a plain-English profile. It reads the summary back to you and asks if you want to change anything. This is the moment to say yes if something felt off.

You're done. If you ever want to change your brand, say Change my brand, and it shows you what's saved and asks what to update. It never makes you start over.

Your next step is the next video: bring your footage and say Make my video.

**Notes for editing:** the waiting while it checks things gets cut. I add a zoom on each preview image so viewers can read it.

## Tutorial 3: Add your footage and voiceover

**Format:** SCREEN. **Length:** about 6 minutes.

**Have ready**
- One short clip of you talking, 30 to 60 seconds. Keep your first tests under about 2 minutes.
- One short voiceover recording for the explainer part.
- A Google Drive, Dropbox or OneDrive account, to show the big-file method.
- Your project folder open, so viewers can see the raw folder.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. Your assistant can't edit what it can't reach. This video shows you the two ways to get your video and your voiceover to it.

First, how big a clip should you start with? Thirty to sixty seconds is ideal. Keep your early tests under about two minutes. You can go longer once you trust the process.

Method one is the easiest, and it's the one I use. If your assistant runs on your own computer, which means Claude Code or Codex on your machine, you don't upload anything. Open your project folder. Inside it there's a folder for raw media. [pause] Drag your video into that folder. Then tell your assistant the file name. That's it. Nothing leaves your computer.

Method two is for when your assistant runs in the cloud. That means Claude Code on the web, a Codex cloud task, or something you started from your phone. A cloud assistant can't see your computer's files, and chat attachments have size limits that most talking-head videos blow past.

So you put the video in a cloud folder. Upload it to Google Drive, Dropbox or OneDrive. [pause] Now set sharing so anyone with the link can view it. In Drive, click Share, change the access to Anyone with the link, and copy the link. [pause]

Paste the link into the chat and say: Download this video into my project and edit it.

Two things to know. First, when the edit is done, go back and set sharing back to Restricted. Don't leave a video open to anyone who has the link. Second, a cloud assistant sometimes can't reach the file host. If the download is blocked, the assistant tells you which web addresses to allow in your environment settings, and the Start Here file lists them.

Now your voiceover. For an explainer, the voiceover is the timing for the whole video. The recording matters more than a written script, because the plug-in lines up the pictures with the words you actually said. A script alone can't tell it when each word happens.

So record in a quiet room, speak at your normal pace, and keep one take that you're happy with. Save it as an audio file and put it in the raw media folder the same way, or share it by link. [pause]

Then tell the assistant what it is: This is my voiceover. Turn it into an explainer. You'll see that in its own video.

One last tip. Don't paste passwords or private keys into the chat, ever. We'll talk about that again in the stock footage video.

Quick recap. Small or local: drop it in the raw folder. Cloud assistant: share by link, then set it back to Restricted. Start short. I'll see you in the next video, where we make your first edit.

## Tutorial 4: Make my video

**Format:** SCREEN, one real session. I cut the waiting. **Length:** about 8 minutes.

**Have ready**
- Your brand already set up (Tutorial 2).
- A 30 to 60 second talking-head clip in your raw folder.
- Your script or notes, if you have them, to compare against the transcript.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. This is the video you've been waiting for. Your clip goes in, a finished video comes out.

Your clip is already in the raw folder. I'm going to type three words: Make my video. [pause]

Before it builds anything, it listens to your clip and writes out everything you said. This is called the transcript. It shows it to you and asks you to check it. Don't skip this. Look for your name, any brand names, links, and your last line. Voice-to-text sometimes spells a name two different ways, and sometimes it invents a word after the audio ends. [pause] If something is wrong, tell it, and it fixes it.

It also checks the ending of your take. If your clip is cut off in the middle of a sentence, it tells you before it edits anything, so you don't find out at the end.

Now it plans the video using your saved style and your saved brand. It follows a set of editing rules that I built from weeks of real edits. A change on screen at least every four seconds. You stay full frame for about four seconds at a time, six at most. Nothing sits on your face. Text stays clear of the places where the app buttons live. Effects land on the exact word you say, not a guess.

Then comes the part I love. Before it renders anything, it makes a storyboard. That's your whole video as numbered pictures, in order, with the words being said under each one. [pause] You look at it, and you either say Approve, or you name the numbers you want changed. Frame 6 is too busy, make it simpler. Frame 9 covers my face. It changes only those parts and makes a new storyboard. Nothing renders until you say yes.

There's also a built-in checker. It looks for captions that cover a graphic, captions that repeat what's already on screen, and effects that land on the wrong word. It fixes what it finds or tells you why something stays.

Now you approve and it renders. A video usually takes 30 minutes to an hour, and longer videos take longer. You don't have to sit and watch it. Film your next one, answer email, take a walk.

When it's done, it checks the file. The length, the size, whether there's sound. Then it hands you the video. It will also say what it could not check. It can look at pictures but it can't hear. So you listen to the music and the voice yourself.

If you want a change, ask for it in plain words. Make the captions bigger. Move them to the top. Try the Spotlight style. It never overwrites your old video. It makes a new version and keeps the first one.

And if you have several clips, you can ask for a batch. It makes them one after another, and if one fails, it tells you which and keeps going with the rest.

It never posts for you and never schedules anything. The finished video is yours to post.

**Notes for editing:** I cut the waiting, zoom into the storyboard sheet, and add a callout on the approve step.

## Tutorial 5: Styles, effects and music

**Format:** VOICE. **Length:** about 7 minutes.

**I make:** the same short clip rendered in each of the six styles plus the yap style, side by side. A tour of effect examples from the effect library. One finished video before and after the music is added.

**You record:** the voiceover below. Nothing else.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. You never have to start from a blank page. This video shows you how to choose a look, how effects work, and how to add music.

Let's start with styles. A style is a saved bundle of choices. Here is one clip in six styles.

Signature is my everyday look. Kinetic editorial captions and the metal trim from your brand. [pause] Quiet Authority is calm and premium, with pill-style captions, a plain black background and soft music. Spotlight is dramatic. Heavy captions, velvet, a copper outline and big moments. Show and Tell is for teaching. Your points appear as you say them, with stacks, checklists and callouts. Hype Reel is fast and loud, with orbit and wall effects and full sound. And Raw Tape is unpolished and real, with pill captions, grain and no music.

There's a seventh, called Yap, for casual videos. It gets its own video.

How do you pick? Say it in chat. Make this one Spotlight. Done.

You can also mix. Say: Make this one Spotlight, but keep my Signature captions. Behind every style are separate dials: the captions, the background, the finish, the color look, the music level, the sound, the outline, and the effects. A style just sets all of them at once. You can change one without losing the rest.

Now effects. There are more than 100 in the library. Animated text and cards. Zooms and camera moves. Spotlights. Orbiting elements. Clones. Phone screens and checklist graphics. Charts. Transitions. End cards. [pause] You do not pick them from a menu. Your assistant reads your script, finds the moments that deserve one, and places each effect on the exact word.

It follows a few rules. One strong effect every four to six seconds is plenty. No effect sits on your face. And it uses effects that add something instead of repeating the caption.

Some effects put you in front of the graphic, like a type wall behind you. Those need a cutout of you. The default cutout is quick. If the edge isn't clean, the assistant can use a higher-quality cutout. It needs a one-time download, and it takes longer, so it only does it for the seconds an effect needs. It asks before downloading anything big.

If an effect doesn't look right the first time, that's normal. You preview, you tell it what to change, and it adjusts.

Now music. You can use your own licensed music. Put your track in the music folder and say: Add my music. It lowers the music under your voice, it fades in and out, and it matches the length of your video. [pause] Use only music you own or have the license for. I can't give you a track to redistribute, and I'd never tell you it's fine to use something you found. The assistant can't hear, so you approve the mix by ear.

So that's it. Pick a style, mix it if you like, let the assistant place the effects, and add your own music. I'll see you in the next video.

## Tutorial 6: Edit my yap

**Format:** VOICE. **Length:** about 5 minutes.

**I make:** a before and after of one of your real yaps. The raw clip, then the finished one. A short clip of the command being typed.

**You record:** the voiceover below.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. A yap is a casual talking video. In the car. On the couch. At your desk. It's supposed to feel real, so the edit is light on purpose.

Here's the raw clip. [pause] Now here's the same clip after one command.

The command is: Edit my yap.

What did it do? It added word-by-word captions, with a few key words in color. It trimmed the long pauses. It added a quiet music bed. It put in two or three slow zooms. And it left the room showing, so you still feel like you're in the car.

Let's talk about the pauses. The plug-in tightens every silence longer than a set length. The default is three tenths of a second. If you want looser, ask for four tenths. If you want it snappier, ask for two tenths. [pause] These are jump cuts, so the assistant checks your pose at each cut.

Before it edits, it shows you the transcript and asks you to confirm the words. Check your name and any links.

If you recorded your yap in a few pieces, you can say: Join these clips into one yap. It lines them up, then edits the joined video.

What a yap does not get: no end card, and it ends right after your last line, before your hand reaches for the camera. No stack of effects. A yap should look like a yap, not like a polished talking head.

Keep your original audio. Voice-changing tools can strip out the room sound, and the room sound is what makes a yap feel real.

If it ever says "that's a talking head, not a yap," it will ask you one time which you mean. Pick one, and it keeps going.

A quick note on captions. The captions can look a hair late against a mouth, especially on an AI avatar. If you see a caption that's off, tell the assistant the exact second and the word. That's how it gets fixed.

That's a yap. One command, light edit, real feel.

## Tutorial 7: Animated explainers

**Format:** VOICE. **Length:** about 7 minutes.

**I make:** one short explainer in each of the three styles, and one showing each of the five layouts. One portrait and one landscape example. One example with the stick figure and one with a character, if you have character art ready.

**You record:** the voiceover below.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. Here's how you turn your voice into an animated video, with no camera and nothing to film.

All you need is a voiceover. Your own recording. Say this: Turn my voiceover into an explainer. [pause]

The recording is what everything is timed to. The plug-in reads the words you said and places each picture on the exact word. A written script by itself can't do that, which is why we start from audio.

Now the look. There are three styles. Whiteboard is clean line art. Editorial is led by serif typography. Dark is a dark background with light figures. [pause] Before it builds the whole video, it shows you a still of the style so you can say yes or no.

Next, layouts. These are the ways a scene can be organized, and your assistant picks the one that fits your idea. Compare puts two things side by side. Numbered List walks through steps. Layers stacks ideas. Load versus Capacity shows how much something is carrying against what it can carry. Process or Loop shows a cycle. [pause]

A heads-up on that one. Load versus Capacity needs real numbers from you. The assistant won't invent them. If you don't give it numbers, it picks another layout.

Who is on screen? By default, a stick figure drawn in code, so you need nothing. If you have your own character art, a 2D or 3D character you own, you can use that instead. Give it clear poses, all the same size, on a transparent background, and it builds around them. If you use a character, make sure you own the rights.

You can use portrait for Reels, TikTok and Shorts, or landscape for YouTube. [pause] Here's the same explainer in both.

You can also make a video out of your own images and footage with a voiceover. Same command, same process. You bring the pieces, it builds the timing.

And just like your talking heads, you can change anything with a sentence. Make the figure smaller. Use the dark style. Change the third scene to a list.

Every explainer is a little lesson, and the examples you're seeing are the lesson. Make one this week.

## Tutorial 8: Life Scenes

**Format:** VOICE. **Length:** about 6 minutes.

**I make:** three finished Life Scenes from your approved examples, showing a text-message overlay, a note, and a notification stack. A short clip of each camera move: zoom, pullback, punch, drift and freeze.

**You record:** the voiceover below.

**Say this**

Welcome to lesson eight. This one is called a Life Scene, and I made that name up, so let me tell you exactly what it is. A Life Scene is a few seconds of your life, animated: a still image that moves just enough to show a moment, with a few words of text on it.

Where do the clips come from? Google Flow, Veo, Seedance or Kling, stock footage, or your own camera. If a clip has its own music or speech baked in, listen to it first.

The command is: Make a Life Scene. Then you tell it a few things. A title. The clip or clips. The message of the scene. And whether you have a voiceover.

Here's what I like most about how it works. The assistant writes the on-screen words first, and you approve them before anything is built. The lines stay short, about 22 characters each, two or three lines on a card. They're written the way you talk, so they make sense on a single hearing. If you give it stiff wording from a prompt, it rewrites it in plain English.

Then the camera. Each clip gets one move. A slow zoom pushes in. A pullback starts on a detail and reveals the scene. A punch is a quick zoom on a beat. A drift is a gentle handheld sway. And a freeze holds the frame while your text lands. [pause]

Now the overlays, which are my favorite part. A text message that types out, sends, and gets a reply. A paper note that writes itself line by line. A stack of notification cards. A tilted phone with a list of recordings. A checklist that ticks off, one item at a time. Comments that pop in with a heart. And a timeline that lights up one point at a time. [pause]

Every word inside those overlays comes from you. The names and handles stay generic, never a real person's. That's on purpose.

Life Scenes are vertical, 1080 by 1920. Before it renders, the assistant pulls a frame at every text moment so you can check that nothing is cut off and nothing sits on a face. You approve, and it renders.

Use a Life Scene between your talking points, or on its own. One clip, one message, one feeling.

## Tutorial 9: Stock footage

**Format:** SCREEN. **Length:** about 8 minutes.

**Before you record:** run a full test with your own real keys first. I tested this tool during building, but a live search with your real Pexels and Pixabay keys has not been run on your computer yet. Record this one only after it works for you.

**Have ready**
- Your Pexels and Pixabay accounts, signed in.
- Your project folder open, with the stock keys example file visible.
- A script with at least three shots, so there's something to search for.
- A screen that won't show your keys. **Never show your keys on screen.** Blur or cut that moment.

**Say this**

Hi, it's Dr. CK. Stock footage is optional. Everything works without it. But if you want B-roll to cut away to, here's how to add free clips from Pexels and Pixabay, with your own keys.

A key is like a library card. It lets the plug-in search on your behalf. They're free, and they take about five minutes each. Your keys stay on your computer. I never see them, and they're never part of the plug-in.

Pexels first. Go to pexels.com/api and sign in. Click Your API Key, fill in the short form. The app name can be anything, like My video projects. Copy the key. [pause]

Pixabay next. Go to pixabay.com/api/docs and sign in. Scroll down to Parameters. Your key is shown right there next to the word key. Copy it. [pause]

Now, where do they go? In your project folder, there's a file called stock-keys example. Make a copy of it and name the copy stock-keys.json. Open it. Paste each key between the quotes. [pause] Save it.

Then say to your assistant: Check my stock keys. It tells you "set" for each one it found. It never prints the keys back to you.

One rule I'll repeat. Never paste your keys into the chat, an email, or a shared document. If you think a key got out, make a new one on the website. You can use just one provider. You don't need both.

If your assistant runs in the cloud, you set the keys as environment secrets instead of a file, and you allow a few web addresses. The stock footage guide lists them.

Now let's use it. Say: Find stock footage for this video. First, it reads your script and writes a shot list. Each shot becomes a search phrase of two to four concrete words. What's in the frame, not the idea. "Woman typing laptop night," not "productivity." [pause]

It searches, and shows you results. Each one shows where it's from, how long it is, who made it, and a link to the clip's page. It also saves preview images. [pause] You look at them and open the page links to watch them. You choose.

Only then does it download. It downloads one clip per choice, and nothing else. It saves each clip into your project and records the source and the creator in a credits file. [pause]

When your video is done, say: Show me my stock credits. It gives you a credit line for each clip, ready to paste into your caption or description.

A few rules the tool follows. Pixabay requires results to be saved for 24 hours, so the same search within a day doesn't hit their service again. Clips are downloaded before use, never streamed from the website. And it will never bulk-download or build you a library of clips you aren't using.

Both sites' licenses allow free use in videos, with limits. For anything sensitive, like an ad with a recognizable person or brand, read the license page for yourself. The terms can change, so check them before you publish commercially.

If you hit "too many requests," wait a little and try again. Both sites limit searches.

That's stock footage. Optional, free, and you stay in charge of every clip.

2. **Codex.** The package works in both Claude Code and Codex. Say so plainly in the lessons.
3. **Stock footage.** Run one real search and one real download with your own keys before recording tutorial 9.
